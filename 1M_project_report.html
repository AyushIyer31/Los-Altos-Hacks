A Two-Model, Condition-Aware Machine-Learning Pipeline for Engineering Thermostable PET-Degrading Enzymes

PET-Lab Project Report  ·  Stability-Prediction Track  ·  June 2026
Abstract. Plastics such as PET degrade fastest near 70 °C, yet most plastic-degrading enzymes denature well below that temperature. We present an integrated machine-learning pipeline that pairs two complementary models: (1) a whole-protein screening model that predicts an enzyme's melting temperature (Tm) directly from sequence, used to select the most heat-tolerant starting enzymes; and (2) a mutation model that predicts the stability change (ΔΔG) of a point mutation, used to optimize the chosen enzyme. Both are built on ESM-2 protein language-model embeddings and a zoo of gradient-boosted / ensemble regressors, trained on a 1,001,888-row, leakage-audited stability dataset assembled from seven public sources. We evaluate each model on an independent, homology-clean benchmark: S669 for the mutation model (ensemble Pearson 0.39, 95% CI 0.32–0.46) and a newly-constructed BRENDA temperature-stability test set for the screening model (AUC 0.73, 95% CI 0.71–0.76). The aim is to cut expensive wet-lab effort by filtering and ranking candidates in silico; because only a short shortlist reaches the bench, we prioritize precision and operate at high-precision thresholds. The contribution is one of integration, application, and data rigor rather than a new algorithm.

1 · Introduction & Motivation

Enzymatic recycling of PET (polyethylene terephthalate) is most efficient at elevated temperatures (~70 °C), where the polymer becomes flexible and accessible. The bottleneck is biological: naturally-occurring PET hydrolases (e.g. IsPETase) lose their fold and activity far below this temperature. Engineering enzymes that remain stable and active under harsh temperature and pH is therefore the central problem.

Two distinct questions arise. First, which enzyme should we start from? — a ranking problem over thousands of candidate sequences, the vast majority of which have no measured stability. Second, how do we improve the chosen enzyme? — a design problem over the space of point mutations. These are different questions requiring different models. Our pipeline answers both and chains them together, and — critically — validates each half on data the models never saw.

Goal: reduce expensive wet-lab effort. Characterizing an enzyme's stability experimentally costs days of work and significant money per candidate, so testing every option is infeasible. The pipeline's job is to filter and rank the candidate space in silico and forward only the most promising designs to the bench. For this purpose precision matters more than recall: it is far better to hand the lab a short, trustworthy shortlist (few false positives) than a long list padded with candidates that fail — missing some good enzymes (lower recall) is an acceptable price for not wasting bench time on bad ones. We therefore operate the models at high-precision thresholds, and report the full precision–recall trade-off so the operating point can be chosen deliberately.

2 · The Two-Model Approach

Stage 1 · Screen

Whole-protein Tm model — “the scout”

Input: one sequence. Output: predicted melting temperature (absolute, °C).

Answers “which enzyme is worth our time?” Predicts heat tolerance for enzymes that were never measured in a lab, so a large candidate pool can be ranked in silico and narrowed to the sturdiest scaffolds.

Stage 2 · Optimize

Mutation ΔΔG model — “the engineer”

Input: one sequence + one mutation. Output: stability change (relative, ΔΔG).

Answers “how do we upgrade the one we picked?” Scores candidate point mutations so the stabilizing ones can be combined into an improved design.

The two are complementary because one is absolute and the other is relative. Chained: the screening model gives the starting enzyme's Tm; the mutation model gives the boost from chosen mutations; together they estimate the engineered enzyme's final stability (Tm(design) ≈ Tm(WT) + ΔTm(mutations)) — an absolute number you cannot obtain from the mutation model alone. Only the top designs proceed to wet-lab confirmation.

① CandidatesMany enzyme sequences, most with no measured stability.
② Screen · Stage 1Tm model ranks them → pick the sturdiest scaffold.
③ Optimize · Stage 2ΔΔG model scores mutations → keep the stabilizing ones.
④ CombineTm(WT)+ΔTm → estimated stability of the engineered design.
⑤ ConfirmLab-test only the top-ranked designs.

3 · Pipeline Stages

Both models share the same four-stage skeleton — data preparation → feature engineering → training → validation — but differ in input granularity (whole protein vs. mutation pair).

3.1 Data preparation

  • Shared staging. Seven public sources are merged into one richly-annotated table (build_staging_table.py) with full provenance per row (sequence, mutation, T, pH, measurement type, source, PMID). A strict leakage audit removes exact duplicates and any row overlapping the S669 test set — including sequence-homology overlap detected with mmseqs2 (≥30% identity, ≥50% coverage), which exact-key matching misses.
  • Stage 2 (mutation): keep only ΔΔG rows that have both a wild-type and a mutant sequence (508,693 rows).
  • Stage 1 (screening): keep Tm rows (single sequence → temperature); 23,349 unique sequences from Meltome + FireProtDB.

3.2 Feature engineering

Both use ESM-2 (esm2_t30_150M, 640-dim, mean-pooled per residue) — a protein language model that encodes evolutionary and structural context from sequence alone (no 3-D structure required).

Stage 2 — mutation (276-dim)

  • Embeddings of [wt, mut, mut−wt] → PCA to 256 dims (keeps 500K+ rows tractable).
  • Engineered block (not PCA'd): ESM-2 ΔLL (log-likelihood ratio of the substitution, the mutation-local signal); 12-d biochemical Δ (charge, volume, hydrophobicity, H-bonds, polarity, aromaticity, Pro/Gly); assay conditions (T, pH).

Stage 1 — screening (641-dim)

  • Whole-protein ESM-2 embedding (640-d) + log(sequence length).
  • No PCA (the Tm set is small enough to use full embeddings).

3.3 Training

Both stages train a model zoo of gradient-boosted and ensemble regressors (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Random Forest, Extra Trees, MLP) and average them into an ensemble. Embeddings are computed once on GPU (cached) on the Nautilus / NRP Kubernetes cluster; the regressors train on CPU. Splits are protein-grouped so no wild-type sequence appears on both sides of the train/validation boundary.

3.4 Validation

Each model is validated twice: an internal grouped held-out set, and an external, homology-audited benchmark the model never saw — S669 for the mutation model and the BRENDA temperature-stability set for the screening model (Section 4.2).

4.2 Test data (independent benchmarks)

S669 — mutation model

669 single-point mutations with experimental ΔΔG; the field-standard benchmark for stability-change prediction. Audited (exact + homology) against the training set so no train mutation or close homolog leaks in.

BRENDA temperature-stability set — screening model (new in this work)

We constructed a fresh whole-protein benchmark from the BRENDA 2026.1 bulk release (CC BY 4.0; 8,129 EC entries, 111,901 protein records). Three steps:

  1. Extract (convert_brenda.py): keep proteins with a pH- and/or temperature-stability annotation. BRENDA stores no sequences inline — only UniProt accessions — so sequences are resolved from UniProt. → 3,060 enzymes (2,886 temperature, 1,176 pH, 1,002 both; 2,917 accessions, 1,229 organisms, 868 EC numbers; 46% hydrolases, the family of PET-degrading enzymes).
  2. Parse (parse_brenda_temp_stability.py): convert the free-text temperature-stability annotations (e.g. “70 [10 min, stable]”, “Tm 76.8 °C”) into a numeric ceiling and a label (positive ≥60 °C / negative ≤45 °C / ambiguous). A heuristic separates true Tm, “stable-up-to”, and inactivation evidence, and discards cold-storage, sub-ambient, immobilized, and implausible values. Parsed 84.8% (2,446).
  3. Leakage audit (brenda_leakage_audit.py): remove any protein overlapping the training set by accession, exact sequence, or mmseqs2 homology (≥30% id, ≥50% cov). Removed 412 (16.8%) — of which 406 were homology-only, again invisible to exact keys.
Final clean BRENDA test set: 2,034 enzymes (979 positive / 584 negative / 471 ambiguous) → 1,563 confident binary cases, provably free of exact and homology overlap with training.

5 · Results

Results follow the pipeline order: the screening model (Stage 1, run first) then the mutation model (Stage 2).

5.1 Stage 1 — Screening model on BRENDA

Internal held-out validation: Tm predicted within RMSE ≈ 6.5 °C (val Pearson ≈ 0.80 across models) — the model learned “sequence → heat tolerance”.

Independent BRENDA test (1,563 unseen enzymes): AUC = 0.732 (95% CI 0.708–0.755)  |  regression Pearson 0.54 (95% CI 0.50–0.57; 0.60 on the true-Tm subset). The tight interval (n=1,563) places the model clearly above chance on proteins — and protein families — it never trained on.

6 · Limitations

  • Modest absolute accuracy. S669 Pearson (0.44 best) is below specialized structure-aware ΔΔG methods (~0.5–0.6); the BRENDA AUC (0.73) is a useful-but-not-strong screener. Both are tree-on-embedding baselines, not fine-tuned language models.
  • Class imbalance. Both benchmarks are imbalanced, so F1 must be read alongside AUC; “best-F1” thresholds lean toward recall.
  • Label noise in BRENDA. The numeric ceiling is parsed from free text and mixes true Tm with “stable-up-to” values; a basis flag allows filtering to the cleaner subset.
  • pH not yet modeled. The screening model currently predicts temperature stability; pH-stability annotations are extracted but not yet numeric.

7 · Conclusion — Why the Integration Is Novel

The individual components here are established: language-model embeddings, ΔΔG prediction, and sequence-to-Tm prediction all exist in the literature. The novelty is in the integration, the application, and the data rigor — i.e. it is a systems contribution:

  • Two complementary models in one loop. An absolute screening model and a relative mutation model are combined into a single screen-then-optimize pipeline that produces an estimated absolute stability for a designed enzyme — neither model achieves this alone.
  • Application focus. The pipeline targets thermostable PET-degrading enzymes specifically, where the training data is enriched (46% hydrolases) and the goal (function at ~70 °C) is concrete.
  • A new, reusable benchmark. We repurpose BRENDA's free-text stability annotations into a numeric, labeled, homology-clean independent test set — a use of BRENDA not previously available for whole-protein stability evaluation.
  • Leakage discipline. Both benchmarks are audited for sequence-homology leakage with mmseqs2, not just exact-key overlap. In this project that step alone removed 130k+ training rows and ~17% of the BRENDA test set — overlap that would otherwise silently inflate every reported score.

In short: rather than a new algorithm, the project delivers a carefully-integrated, condition-aware, leakage-audited pipeline that turns two known prediction tasks into a usable enzyme-engineering workflow, with honest, independent validation on both halves.
